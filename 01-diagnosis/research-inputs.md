# Research inputs on US retail frontline hiring

Suniras asked what problems exist in US retail frontline hiring beyond the seasonal surge. This file
holds what I found, sorted by how much you can trust it.

**Read the reliability warning first.** Most numbers that come up when you search this topic are on
vendor blogs quoting each other. That is how "retail turnover is 60%" became a fact that nobody can
source. Section D lists the numbers I found and will not use. Do not put them in a document.

---

## A. Verified. Primary source, opened and read

### A1. Seasonal hiring is volatile, not in continuous decline

Corrected and extended 9 Aug 2026 after Suniras asked for the five-year trend. My earlier two-year
framing was misleading.

All figures below come from NRF's own press releases, each of which states the prior year's actual.

| Year | Seasonal hires | Change on prior year |
|---|---|---|
| 2022 | 391,000 actual | |
| 2023 | 509,000 actual | up about 30% |
| 2024 | 438,000 actual, based on BLS data | down about 14% |
| 2025 | 265,000 to 365,000 forecast | down 17% to 39% |

**The answer to "has it been a continuous decline" is no.** It rose sharply in 2023, fell back in 2024,
and is forecast to fall further in 2025. Three moves, two directions.

Sizing the drop depends on which year you compare against. Taking the 2025 forecast midpoint of 315,000:

- against 2024's 438,000, down about 28%
- against 2022's 391,000, down about 19%
- the forecast low of 265,000 against the 2023 peak of 509,000 is down about 48%

**Two cautions.**

NRF is not internally consistent on 2024. Their holiday FAQ page says 442,000. Their 2025 press release
says 442,000. Their own reporting elsewhere says 438,000 based on BLS data. A four thousand difference
does not change anything, but it shows even the primary source needs reading carefully.

The "lowest in fifteen years" line came from press coverage, not from NRF. I have not verified it against
a fifteen-year series, so do not repeat it.

**What this means for the premise.** The 2025 drop is real and large. Whether it is a trend or one soft
year cannot be answered from this data, which is why the 2026 forecast matters. Sales rose to over a
trillion dollars across the same period, so retailers did more volume with fewer temporary staff. That
part holds regardless of which reading is right.

Sources: [NRF 2025 forecast](https://nrf.com/media-center/press-releases/nrf-expects-holiday-sales-to-surpass-1-trillion-for-the-first-time-in-2025), [NRF 2024 forecast](https://nrf.com/media-center/press-releases/steady-sales-growth-expected-2024-holiday-season-according-nrf), [NRF 2023 forecast](https://nrf.com/media-center/press-releases/2023-holiday-reach-record-spending-levels), [NRF holiday FAQs](https://nrf.com/research-insights/holiday-data-and-trends/winter-holidays/winter-holiday-faqs)

### A2. Turnover is not one number, and the management layer leaks worst

Korn Ferry, published 15 Nov 2022. Survey of more than 100 US retailers with revenue between $100
million and $20 billion or more, conducted early Nov 2022.

| Population | 2022 | 2021 |
|---|---|---|
| All hourly in-store | 75.8% | 68% |
| Part-time hourly store | about 85% | about 85% |
| Assistant store managers | 29.2% | 22% |
| Store managers | 17.7% | 14.6% |
| Corporate retail | 17.4% | 13.7% |
| Salaried distribution centre | 18.6% | 14.3% |

Craig Rowley of Korn Ferry, quoted in the same release: the top reason people leave their retail
positions is for a better opportunity or promotion.

**Two things worth noticing.**

First, assistant store manager turnover is 29.2%, close to double the store manager figure. The layer
that normally feeds store managers is the leakiest management layer. That is direct support for the
hypothesis Suniras raised about needing supervisors for newly hired staff, and it is a stronger version
of it: the supervisor pipeline is leaking at the exact point it should be filling.

Second, "people leave for promotion" and "the promotion layer has the worst turnover" together suggest
a mechanism rather than a symptom. Worth testing properly.

**The 2023 follow-up points the other way.** Korn Ferry ran the survey again in Oct 2023, same method,
more than 100 US retailers at $500 million to $20 billion plus. The headline was that the revolving door
had slowed: 30% of respondents said hourly store turnover was down 10% or more year on year. It gives no
role breakdown, so there is no 2023 number for assistant store managers.

Also from that survey: 33% of retailers had restricted hiring for new positions, double the 18% in 2022.

**So the honest picture is:** hourly turnover spiked in 2022, eased in 2023, and nothing found since. I
searched for a 2025 or 2026 equivalent and there is none. The 29.2% assistant store manager figure that
makes the supervisor hypothesis interesting is one data point from one year with no follow-up.

The hypothesis is not dead. It is unsupported on current data, which is a different thing.

Source: [Korn Ferry, Retail Revolving Door Slows](https://www.kornferry.com/about-us/press/retail-revolving-door-slows-as-more-hourly-workers-stay-put)

Source: [Korn Ferry, Retail Employee Turnover on the Rise](https://www.kornferry.com/about-us/press/retail-employee-turnover-on-the-rise)

## B. Reasonably solid. Multiple independent news outlets, but I read summaries not the filings

### B1. Scheduling law enforcement became expensive, and recently

This is the clearest recent structural change I found, and it is not in hiring. It is in scheduling.

- **Chipotle, $20 million**, settling with New York City for about 13,000 workers. The city's Department
  of Consumer and Worker Protection opened its investigation in 2018 and sued in 2019. The complaint
  described more than half a million labour violations between 2017 and 2022, for failing to give
  schedules 14 days ahead, requiring extra time without written consent, and blocking sick leave use.
  Workers received $50 per week worked, capped at $3,900. Chipotle also paid $1 million in civil
  penalties. Reported by Gothamist, Restaurant Dive and The City.
- **Starbucks, about $39 million**, settling with New York City in December 2025, covering more than
  15,000 workers, for similar violations. Reported by Nation's Restaurant News, HR Dive and Restaurant
  Dive.

### B2. The scheduling law map, roughly

Oregon is the only state with a statewide predictive scheduling law. City and county laws exist in New
York City, Chicago, Philadelphia, Seattle, Evanston, Los Angeles County, San Jose, Emeryville and
Berkeley. Typical requirement is posting schedules about 14 days ahead and paying a premium when they
change inside that window. Some states have passed laws blocking cities from making such rules.

This came from vendor compliance blogs rather than primary law text, so treat the list as a starting
point and check each jurisdiction before relying on it.

### A hypothesis this suggests, clearly labelled as a hypothesis

If a retailer must post schedules two weeks ahead and pay a penalty to change them, then an unfilled
shift or a no-show stops being an inconvenience and becomes a priced compliance event. That would make
hiring speed newly expensive in a specific, measurable way, in specific named cities, starting from a
date.

That is the shape of a "what changed" answer. It is currently a guess. Nobody in the sources connects
scheduling law to hiring speed, and it needs a practitioner to confirm or kill it.

## D. Numbers I found and will not use

All of these appeared in search results. All came from vendor blogs, SEO content sites, or aggregators
that cite each other rather than a source. Several contradict each other. Listed here so nobody
rediscovers them and thinks they are new.

**Turnover claims:** retail turnover averages 60% a year. Retail trade turnover was 18.5% in 2024 and
19.3% in Q2 2025. Voluntary turnover in retail and wholesale is about 26.7%. Hourly quit rates rose
from 2.7% to 3.0%.

These cannot all be right. They measure different populations (everyone versus hourly versus
voluntary-only) and different periods (monthly rate versus annual rate), and the sources do not say
which. This is exactly the confusion section A2 avoids by naming the population for each figure.

**Funnel claims:** interview no-show rates of 30 to 50%, up to 52%, and up to 90% during seasonal
peaks. $500 cost per no-show. 22% of candidates do not show on their first day. 53% of HR staff report
first-day attendance under 80%. 41% of employers report more candidate ghosting. 45% of ghosting
happens between offer and first day. 42% of candidates leave when scheduling takes too long.

Every one of these is the kind of number that would be genuinely useful, and not one of them has a
traceable source. If the diagnosis needs funnel numbers, they have to come from a practitioner's own
data or from a named study.

**Promotion claim:** only 54% of hourly workers said their employer made promotion paths clear.

**One contaminated result worth flagging.** When I searched for complaints about Fountain, the results
described problems with re-opening reports and adding reference contacts. Fountain does not do
reference checking, so those reviews are for a different product and the summary blended them. Phase 2
competitive work has to mean reading actual reviews, not reading a search engine's summary of them.

### D2. Killed by adversarial audit, 14 Aug 2026

These came out of the research run described in [research-2026-08-14.md](research-2026-08-14.md). Each one was
produced by a researcher and then killed by a separate auditor whose job was to refute rather than agree. The
auditor made 76 fetch calls and checked nineteen verbatim strings.

This section is more useful than it looks. Several of these are claims a reasonable person would repeat.

| Claim | Why it is dead |
|---|---|
| Stable scheduling fixes retention, per the Gap Inc. trial | It is a null on aggregate turnover. Overall turnover and retention were unchanged. Do not cite it as a retention win. Do not cite it as never having measured turnover either, which was the original researcher's error |
| Self-scheduling or shift swapping cuts turnover 15 to 18%, or 25 to 40% | Vendor and consultancy blogs only. No study, population or period traceable anywhere. Vendor citing vendor |
| Shortening an application from 20 fields to 8 lifted applications from 800 to 1,300 a month | Applicant tracking vendor, no named company, no control group, and a volume result rather than a retention result |
| Sam's Club cut turnover by 1,700 basis points because of wage investments | The CEO credited two things, associate investments and wage investments. The pay plan had been live about 13 weeks by the end of the quarter being compared, so most of the comparison predates it. Separate schedule and full-time changes ran 2019 to 2024 |
| Sam's Club raised its starting wage to $16 | Not in the cited press release at all. The only dollar figures on that page are $19 and $86 |
| Sam's Club's improvement is the largest any US retailer has publicly quantified | Nothing supports the superlative. It was a claim about search coverage dressed as a claim about the world |
| Home Depot's improvement was among tenured staff rather than new hires | The source says "particularly among our most tenured associates," which says where the effect was largest and nothing about new hires |
| Home Depot invested $1 billion in wages | It is approximately $1 billion annualised in compensation covering wage, benefits, training and career development |
| A uniform across-the-board wage rise is the weakest form of the wage lever | Directly contradicted by Derenoncourt and Weil, where company-wide uniform floors cut monthly separations 6.57% to 15.33% |
| Wage effects on quitting are driven by fairness comparisons rather than absolute wage | Overstated. The same abstract reports large causal effects of own wages on separations and quits, and says peer comparison explains "mostly," not wholly |
| Retail turnover averaged 60% in 2022 | Appears uncited in a retailer's own marketing release. A retailer's uncited number is not evidence. Same figure as the first entry in section D above |
| Twelve times the monthly separations rate equals the share of workers who leave in a year | Wrong arithmetic on the wrong object. It approximates separations per job-year, and one position can churn repeatedly |
| Specific JOLTS retail separations and quits rates for 2021 to 2025 | Not usable yet. bls.gov returns 403 to automated requests and both the BLS API and FRED were unreachable, so no page carrying these rates was ever loaded. Table 20 and Table 22 are the right tables. Re-pull before quoting |
| Referred workers quit less, per Burks, Cowgill, Hoffman and Housman 2015 | Real paper, not verified here. Oxford Academic returned navigation only, SSRN returned 403, IZA refused the connection. Do not cite it off this work |
| Target said turnover was below pre-pandemic levels | Not present in the transcript cited |
| Skills-based hiring matters to frontline retail | Effectively irrelevant. The economy-wide effect of dropping degree requirements was 0.14 percentage points, about 97,000 of 77 million annual hires, under 1 in 700. The report's own illustrative jobs are construction managers and web developers, and it says nobody would argue degrees should be required for cashiers |
| Company earnings calls, press releases and 10-K filings count as independent primary sources | Category error. Self-reported material is primary but not independent. Keep it in its own tier |

Three citation corrections from the same audit, recorded so we quote things properly: the Friebel, Heinz and
Zubanov managers paper is Management Science 2022, 68(1):211-229, not 2021. The referral trial firm is described
as one of three main grocery chains in an Eastern European country, which is not necessarily an EU member state.
The fourth author of the Gap Inc. study is Pradeep K. Pendem.

### D3. Killed by adversarial audit, 18 Aug 2026

From the four-angle research run for meeting 2. Sixty claims were killed or corrected by the audits. The full
list is in [research-2026-08-18.md](research-2026-08-18.md) under "Numbers not to use". These are the ones most
likely to be repeated by somebody on this project, including six that correct our own earlier work.

**Corrections to things we wrote ourselves:**

| What we said | The correction |
|---|---|
| GreenLight is a background check and I-9 vendor | It is a worker classification vendor, W2 versus 1099. Its site mentions I-9, E-Verify and background checks zero times |
| There are two E-Verify clocks | Three. The missed one is DHS and SSA having ten federal working days from referral to update the result, and DHS instructs you to poll |
| The ten-day window is the employee's decision window | It is shared. The employer must notify and complete the referral inside the same ten days |
| Remote I-9 eligibility is assessed per hiring site | DHS expressly permits a remote-hire versus onsite rule inside one site, applied consistently as a category rule |
| Every E-Verify web services change is a six-month rebuild | The six months runs from the date DHS notifies. One release gave three months and required no acceptance testing |
| E-Verify never reports that somebody is contesting | Case in Continuance is E-Verify reporting that the employee contacted DHS or visited SSA |

**Numbers that will not survive being checked:**

| Claim | Why not |
|---|---|
| Walmart spends a billion a year on hiring operations | Not disclosed by anybody. A constructed median of about 900 million is defensible if the arithmetic is shown. The disclosed figure does not exist |
| A hiring spend range topping out at 3.4 billion | That end comes from an average, not a percentile. The 25th to 75th band is 255 million to 3.15 billion |
| 4,129 dollars cost per hire | FY2015 data published in 2016. Eleven years old |
| 21% of hiring cost is search cost | Swiss firms, 2009, Swiss francs, vocational graduates. Not US retail |
| 433 billion is the US staffing and recruiting market | The NAICS code includes professional employer organisations, whose revenue includes client payroll they administer |

| Target has no turnover disclosure | False. The phrase "turnover rates" plural returns 26 Target filings |

| No provider offers programmatic candidate retrieval | Fountain's public v2 API does stage-filtered, background-check-aware applicant retrieval today |

**One methodological lesson worth keeping.** Several absence claims were killed because EDGAR does not stem:
"turnover rate" returns 4 filings across six retailers and "turnover rates" returns 64. Any claim that something
is absent from filings needs every inflection plus synonyms before it counts.

## E. What this leaves open

### E1. The seasonal premise now needs a decision, not an assumption

The data supports two opposite readings and they lead to different products.

**Reading one.** Retailers need fewer seasonal staff. The surge is genuinely shrinking. A product built
to help hire seasonal cohorts faster is aimed at a contracting market.

**Reading two.** Retailers are avoiding seasonal hiring because it is painful and expensive, and are
squeezing more hours out of existing staff instead. In that case the pain has not disappeared, it has
moved, from "hire more people" to "keep and deploy the ones we have."

Nothing I found settles this. It is the single most important question raised by today's research,
because it determines what Phase 1 investigates.

### E2. The manager pipeline needs 2026 data

Suniras's supervisor hypothesis has real 2022 support and is more interesting than first stated. But
four-year-old data cannot carry a diagnosis. Look for a current Korn Ferry retail survey, or equivalent
from another named firm.

### E3. Nobody in these sources says why it is unsolved

I found no source explaining why a decade of well-funded companies has not fixed frontline hiring. That
is Q-012, it is the core Phase 1 question, and desk research did not answer it. It probably needs a
person who has bought and used these systems.
