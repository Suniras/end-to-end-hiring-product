# Who sits between the store manager and head office

## The caveat, first

**This is tentative, not verified.** Most of it comes from search result summaries and job-description
aggregator sites rather than pages I fetched and read. Several attempts to fetch primary sources failed on
network errors, including Kohl's own careers page and two real job postings.

The one primary page that did load turned out to be a Walmart distribution centre role, not a store role, so
it says nothing about stores.

Job postings also describe what a company intends a role to do, which is not the same as what the person
actually does. So treat everything below as a shape to test rather than a fact to build on.

---

## The short answer to Q-027

**A level does exist, but it is split into two roles, and neither one holds both volume and authority on its
own.**

That is a more awkward answer than a simple yes, and it is more useful.

### Role one: the district manager, who owns the money

Operations. At Kohl's this covers 15 to 20 stores.

What matters most: the role is accountable for "driving sales, profit and productivity while meeting payroll,
expense and shortage goals."

**So the district manager owns the payroll budget.** If store managers cannot hire because they have not been
given enough payroll hours, this is the person who controls that constraint.

On hiring, the district manager "help[s] hire store managers" and "assist[s] store managers in hiring and
firing decisions." Note what that says and does not say. They hire **managers**. They assist with hourly
hiring rather than run it.

### Role two: field HR, who owns the hourly hiring process

Titles vary: Field HR Business Partner, HR Business Partner Field, Associate HR Business Partner Retail. At
Walmart the equivalent appears to be a Market HR Manager sitting above a store-level People Lead.

What these postings say the role does, drawn from several aggregated together:

- Serves as the primary HR contact for a group of assigned stores
- Supports hiring, onboarding and training of hourly associates, in partnership with talent acquisition and
  retail operations
- Partners with retail leaders on scheduling, workforce planning and seasonal staffing needs
- Works directly with district managers, store leaders and hourly staff

**So this is the level where hourly hiring volume aggregates.** Several stores, one person, and the same
person also touches scheduling and workforce planning.

---

## Why the split matters more than the existence

Suniras's original observation was that a store manager has authority but too little volume, while central
talent acquisition has volume but no authority. The field level does not resolve that cleanly. It divides it.

| | District manager | Field HR business partner |
|---|---|---|
| Covers multiple stores | Yes | Yes |
| Controls payroll hours | **Yes** | Partners on it |
| Runs hourly hiring | Assists | **Yes** |
| Measured on | Sales, profit, productivity, payroll, shrink | Not established. Needs sourcing |

Two consequences worth thinking about, and both are mine rather than the sources':

**The person who owns the constraint is not the person who owns the process.** If payroll hours are what
binds, the district manager holds the lever and field HR holds the workflow. A hiring product would sit with
field HR while the thing limiting hiring sits with the district manager.

**The district manager's incentives are not hiring.** Sales, profit, productivity, payroll and shrink.
Hiring appears as a payroll cost line. A product that makes hiring easier does not obviously help any measure
this person is paid on, and might make one of them worse if it fills roles faster.

That second point, if it holds up, is a sharper version of the adoption risk than the store-manager version.
It would mean the operations chain has a reason to be indifferent, not just too busy.

---

## What this does not tell us

- **How many stores a field HR business partner actually covers.** Nothing found. This is the number that
  decides whether the volume is enough for a tool to be worth using.
- **What field HR is measured on.** Not established anywhere. Without it we cannot say whether they would
  want this.
- **Whether either role holds budget.** Influence and process ownership are not the same as signing.
- **Whether smaller retailers have this level at all.** Everything above comes from large chains. A retailer
  with 400 stores may run three regions and no field HR function, which would change the answer completely.
  That matters because NuAisle does not sell to big-box, so mid-market is our likely target.

That last gap is the important one. The whole finding may describe a structure our actual customer does not
have.

---

## How to firm this up

Better than more searching: read the careers pages and 10-K filings of three or four named mid-market US
retailers directly and count what field roles exist and what they say they own. That is a real piece of work
and it would move this from tentative to verified.

A running research angle is separately looking at who controls payroll hours at US retailers and whether that
is central or local. If it comes back with primary sources, it should either strengthen or correct the
district manager half of this.
