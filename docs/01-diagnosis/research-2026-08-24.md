# Research run four: the questions the Target conversation was carrying

24 August 2026. Fourth research run on this project.

**Note on this document.** An earlier version of this file, written the same day, reported findings taken from a
mid-run snapshot before the adversarial pass had finished. That pass then killed thirteen of them. This version
carries only what survived. What was removed is listed at the bottom, because the pattern in the removals is
the most useful thing here.

---

## Why this run happened

Nishant could not arrange the conversation with his colleagues in hiring at Target. That route was carrying
seven facts nothing published answers, and it was the only named route that existed.

So this run went at those questions directly. Six questions, six search angles, 24 sources fetched and read,
110 claims extracted, 25 taken through an adversarial pass whose job was to refute them. **Thirteen were
killed.** Eleven more were dropped before verification when the run hit its budget, so they are recorded as
unverified rather than as findings.

**Sourcing rules, unchanged.** A claim needs a URL that loaded. SEC filings, BLS and Census data, court and
agency enforcement documents, live employer job postings, government documentation, vendor **technical**
documentation, academic papers with a stated method. Vendor marketing is an assumption. An honest "no public
source exists" counts as an answer.

---

## The headline

**Four of the six questions have no traceable public answer.** Field HR span, the distribution of
application-to-first-shift time, the offer-to-first-shift loss rate, and the number of teams that touch one
hire. All four are `[UNKNOWN]`.

**And the pattern in the failures is the finding.** Nearly every claim that died was an attempt to turn an
absence into an affirmative mechanism: SAP's identity matching as *the* cross-site rehire control, an EEOC case
as proof a rehire flag is operationally enforced, TJX's sales figure as a store count, an Oracle search
checkbox as a documented failure path. Each one failed verification.

That tells us something more useful than any single number would have. **The operating detail of this domain is
genuinely unpublished rather than merely hard to find.** Anyone planning to rely on public benchmarks here
should know that before they start.

**One caveat governs everything below.** Not one measured or academic source located is from frontline hourly
retail at a $2B+ US retailer. Hoffman, Kahn and Li state explicitly that their job "is not a retail store job".
Becker and colleagues study campus and professional, almost certainly salaried, candidates. Checkr's book of
business skews gig and marketplace. Oracle Hospitality serves restaurants and hotels. **Every tenure, speed and
turnaround figure here becomes an assumption the moment it is applied to Walmart, Kroger or Target.**

## Question 1: how many stores does one field HR person cover?

**`[UNKNOWN]`. No public source states the store or associate span of any field-level HR role at a large US
retailer.**

`[VERIFIED]` The only first-party multi-unit span figure that survived is a **district operations** comparison:
Kohl's careers site states one District Manager "provide[s] leadership for 15-20 stores".

Three things about that number, all of which limit it:

1. It is a **district operations** role, not field HR. It must not be repurposed as field HR span.
2. The page is **evergreen recruitment-template copy**, not a dated requisition, so 15 to 20 is a **stated
   design span rather than a measured average.**
3. The dated requisition itself returns 410.

**What the sub-question about metrics returned: also `[UNKNOWN]`.** The single attempt to extract measurement
language from a live posting was **refuted unanimously**. So we still cannot say what field HR is measured on,
and the earlier inference that hiring speed appears nowhere in their metrics does not stand.

**Two specific traps recorded for next time.** Walmart reuses the title "(USA) Human Resource Manager" across
Stores, Sam's Club and Supply Chain, so title-matching without checking the division field produces false
positives. And the "1.3M associates" string that appears on those pages is company-wide boilerplate, not a span
figure.

**The untried route.** Archived job postings are the most promising remaining source for a field HR span, and
they could not be checked at all this round because the archive was unreachable from the research environment.
That is worth one manual attempt before concluding the number does not exist anywhere.

## Question 2: how long from application to first paid shift?

**One employer publishes a figure. The distribution, which is what we actually needed, is `[UNKNOWN]`.**

**Correction, 25 Aug 2026.** An earlier version of this section called the Chipotle figure the first
application-to-start number the project had found. That was wrong. **We already had it as E-047 in EVIDENCE.md**,
recorded on 11 August from Workday's acquisition release, correctly tiered as a vendor claim inside an
acquisition announcement, and already carrying the note that Chipotle's turnover rose in 2025 so the two
together test the speed-versus-tenure question. This run added the employer-side source and the caveats below.
It did not add the number.

`[ASSUMPTION]` **Chipotle: an average of four days, down from twelve.** Published in its own newsroom,
February 2025.

Read the caveats before using it, because there are five and they compound:

1. **It is post-automation, not a baseline.** Chipotle attributes the improvement explicitly to its
   Paradox-built AI hiring assistant, launched October 2024. So it is not a picture of unautomated hourly
   hiring, and **one of our two named competitors owns the case study.**
2. **The endpoint is resolved, 25 Aug 2026.** This section previously said the release was internally
   inconsistent, with its lede saying "getting a job offer" and its bullet saying "start on the job", and that
   nobody had disambiguated it. The press release is inconsistent, but the ambiguity is settled elsewhere:
   Chipotle's chief operating officer Jason Kidd is on the record in Fortune, 2 June 2026, saying "the time from
   application to first day on the job has dropped from 12 days to four". **Application to first day on the
   job**, from a named executive. That is the measurement we care about, and it is the better citation.
3. It is a bare mean. No distribution, no sample, no methodology.
4. It appears **nowhere in Chipotle's SEC filings.**
5. It sits alongside **155% reported hourly turnover in 2025.** A speed win with no disclosed quality control.

`[ASSUMPTION]` **Background check turnaround: an average of 2 to 3 days, with a full range from a few hours to
a few weeks.** From Checkr's own integration documentation. Vendor self-report with no methodology or sample,
and Checkr's customer base skews gig and marketplace rather than large retail. The range matters more than the
average: a process with a few-hours floor and a few-weeks ceiling cannot be planned around a mean.

`[VERIFIED NEGATIVE]` **Target's own careers FAQ publishes no timeline at all.** No application-to-start
figure, no time-to-hire, no "hired in X days" claim. The only statement about post-application progress is a
confirmation email. That is negative evidence from the page most likely to carry the number.

**`[UNKNOWN]`: the variance.** No located source, employer or otherwise, discloses the distribution of
application-to-first-shift time. Every figure found is a bare mean, and the variance is the thing that
determines how many accepted candidates are lost before a first shift.

## Question 3: what share of hires never work a first shift?

**`[UNKNOWN]`. No measured figure exists in any located primary source**, for either the never-worked-a-shift
share or the fewer-than-five-shifts share.

`[VERIFIED]` **Home Depot's 10-K is the documented example of the void.** The entire FY2018 filing carries
exactly one workforce-turnover reference and it is purely qualitative. The same filing discloses roughly
413,000 associates, about 29,000 of them salaried, so approximately 384,000 hourly or temporary. That is the
scale of workforce for which no early-attrition figure is disclosed anywhere.

### The adjacent benchmark, and what it cannot be used for

`[ASSUMPTION for retail]` **Hoffman, Kahn and Li (2018), *Quarterly Journal of Economics*.** Across roughly
266,000 low-skill service-sector hires at 15 firms, from payroll records:

- **Median completed job spell: 99 days.**
- **One in six completed spells ends within a month of starting.**
- 79% of spells are completed.
- Post-testing mean completed spell 116 days, standard deviation 138.

Three limits, and the first is the one that would embarrass us if ignored:

**This measures post-start attrition and cannot be used as an offer-to-first-shift loss rate.** The dataset
conditions on people who actually started, so no-shows are structurally absent from it. As a share of all
hires the one-in-six figure is also about 13% rather than 17%, because the histogram covers completed spells
only.

The paper states its job **"is not a retail store job"**: it is data entry, test grading and call centre work.
And the data is pre-2015, which is sound as a historical measurement and stale as a 2026 operating benchmark.

## Question 4: what stops a terminated employee being rehired at another store?

**Partially answered. The mechanism class is documented. Whether any named retailer enforces it across sites is
`[UNKNOWN]`.**

`[VERIFIED]` **Fountain's blocking is two-tiered, and the second tier is opt-in.** From its Hire API
documentation: per-opening restrictions are enforced by default on every create-applicant call, but the
organisation's **additional company-level duplicate rules apply only if the caller passes the optional boolean
`check_if_applicant_is_duplicate`.** An integration that omits the flag creates the applicant without those
checks.

`[VERIFIED]` **And where Fountain does block a returning applicant, the documented mechanism is keyed to the
prior application's rejection reason and to per-opening application limits, not to any termination,
do-not-rehire or rehire-eligibility attribute on the person record.** The documented API surface contains no
such field. The documented company-level example is "Block application if rejection reason is Excess Capacity".

Two limits on that. The rule set is explicitly non-exhaustive, because the docs hedge with "for example" and
Fountain's help domains returned 404, so the full admin rule list could not be enumerated. A
do-not-rehire-flavoured admin rule therefore cannot be ruled out. And this governs the API path; Fountain's
hosted apply flow is not documented on that page.

`[VERIFIED, wrong sector]` **Oracle Hospitality Labor Management implements do-not-rehire as a discrete system
field**, not merely handbook language: a binary "Eligible for Rehire" yes/no on the employee Status tab,
captured as a step inside the termination transaction, with rehire gated on its value **subject to a system
administrator override.**

The sector caveat is the whole caveat. This is the MICROS-derived restaurant and hotel back-office labour
module, Release 8.5, August 2016, and every probe for a current-release equivalent returned 404. It is not
Oracle HCM, and **neither of the two reachable stacks is shown to be used by any retailer in our scope.**

**`[UNKNOWN]`: SAP SuccessFactors.** Both a claim that its control is human-gated identity matching and the
opposing claim that it carries a persistent hard block failed verification. The relevant knowledge-base
articles are behind a login, so nothing about SAP's rehire mechanics is traceable to a loaded page under our
own sourcing rule.

**`[UNKNOWN]`, and this is the important one: whether the control is systematic or manual at any named US
retailer.** No documented failure case, no litigated handbook policy and no cross-site enforcement mechanism at
a named employer survived verification.

**What this means for E1.** The gap our demo dramatises is real as a *mechanism class*: a rehire block that
depends on a flag set at the terminating site and defeasible by an administrator, in a system whose
high-volume-hourly sibling keys its rules to rejection reason instead. What we cannot yet claim is that this
has happened, or that it happens at a particular retailer. The honest framing is a documented weakness in the
mechanism, not a documented failure in the field.

**The decisive evidence, if anybody can get it:** a Workday or iCIMS technical document on rehire-eligibility
gating, or a litigation exhibit showing a for-cause terminee rehired at a second store.

## Question 5: does hiring faster cost you tenure?

**This is the best-evidenced answer in the run, and it is a null.**

`[VERIFIED, non-retail]` **Hiring speed does not predict tenure. Two independent peer-reviewed studies return
nulls.**

In Hoffman, Kahn and Li, hires who started one, two or three months after applying had job durations
statistically indistinguishable from those starting in their application month. **All eighteen coefficients
insignificant, maximum absolute t-statistic 1.38.**

In Becker, Connolly and Slaughter (*Personnel Psychology*), there were **no differences in turnover or in
performance ratings** between employees hired after quicker versus later offers.

`[VERIFIED, non-retail]` **Speed measurably helps at the accept stage.** In a field study of 3,012 candidates
at one large company, job seekers in both populations, 906 students and 2,106 experienced candidates, were more
likely to accept earlier post-interview offers.

### How to say this, and how not to

**This supports "no penalty". It does not support "speed improves retention".** Those are different claims and
only the first one is evidenced. A null result is not a positive one.

So the defensible position is: **compressing the funnel buys acceptance and costs nothing measurable in tenure
or performance.** That is a real commercial argument and it is more than we had. Anything stronger is not
supported.

**Both studies are non-retail or salaried populations**, so this is assumption-grade for hourly retail. It is
also the closest thing to an answer that exists, because no peer-reviewed study regresses calendar time-to-hire
on subsequent tenure for hourly frontline workers at all.

## Question 6: how many teams touch one hire?

**`[UNKNOWN]`, and this is the third run to fail on it.**

Not published. **And no comparable benchmark survived verification either**, including government hiring
process maps with counted owners, retailer sustainability disclosures describing process redesign, vendor case
studies stating a before-state handoff count, and responsibility-matrix language in job postings.

**One thing not to over-read here.** Run four's verifier could not re-establish the federal hiring roadmap as
a comparable benchmark. That is a failure to re-confirm, not a refutation, and it does not cancel run three,
which recomputed the roadmap independently on 18 August: 14 elements, three distinct owner combinations,
thirteen day allotments summing to exactly 80, and ownership changing hands five times at named elements. That
arithmetic is in `research-2026-08-18.md` and it stands. It also already carried the correct caveat, which is
that it is useful as a shape and an order of magnitude and **not transferable to a retailer as a number.**

`[VERIFIED]` The closest thing to evidence is negative and it comes from Target itself. Its hiring-process page
publishes six candidate-facing phases and names Workday as the applicant tracking system, a recruitment team,
an interviewer or panel, and a pre-hire screening process. It also splits accommodation ownership by segment,
with store candidates contacting the store directly and headquarters or supply-chain candidates using a central
address, which is evidence of at least two distinct HR service lanes. **It never publishes a process owner, a
responsibility matrix, or a handoff count.**

it had been used as independent support.

## What is still unknown

Four questions survive all four research runs.

1. **The spread of application-to-first-shift time.** One inconsistent mean exists, from one restaurant
   company, after automation.
2. **The share of accepted offers that never work a first shift.** No measured figure for any US retailer.
3. **The handoff count at a real retailer.** Target's own page is the proof that they do not publish it.
4. **What field HR is measured on**, and therefore whether that person has any reason to care about hiring
   speed.

These are not gaps in the searching. Thirteen killed claims say the same thing from a different direction:
**the operating detail is genuinely unpublished.** No retailer gains by publishing it and every vendor gains by
quoting an unsourced version.

**So desk research on this project is finished.** Recorded as D-023. The four questions move to the design
list, and the first deployment measures them.

Two routes remain if anybody wants to try. One conversation with one practitioner, which is worth more than a
fifth run. And a manual archive retrieval for a field HR requisition, the one route this run could not attempt.

The third route this section used to list, asking Chipotle whether the four days end at orientation or a first
paid shift, is closed: their chief operating officer answered it on the record in Fortune on 2 June 2026, and it
is application to first day on the job.

**One source worth reading that this run surfaced and did not mine.** The iCIMS 2025 State of Frontline Hiring
Report surveys 1,000 US hourly frontline workers and 1,000 frontline hiring managers. That is the population
four research runs have failed to reach, sitting in a published report. It is a vendor survey and it covers four
sectors with no grocery cut, so it is assumption tier. It is still the closest thing to practitioner evidence
this project has found, and it should be read before anybody concludes the desk is exhausted.
