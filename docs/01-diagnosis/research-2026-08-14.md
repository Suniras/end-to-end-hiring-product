# Research, 14 August 2026

## What actually ran, and what did not

Six research angles were launched with hard source rules: fetch every URL, drop any claim whose source does
not load, never cite a search snippet as a source. Each angle was then handed to a separate auditor whose job
was to refute it rather than agree with it.

**Five of the six angles died.** They stalled and were retried six times each before failing. The two that
would have answered "where does the time go" are among the dead: funnel-time and background-checks. Also dead:
onboarding-gap, why-unsolved-structural, and regulatory-clock.

**One angle finished and was audited properly.** It was called what-retailers-did-instead, and it is about what
large US retailers actually spent money on. The audit made 76 fetch calls, checked nineteen verbatim strings,
refuted one claim outright and killed or narrowed about twenty others.

So this is one sixth of a research plan, and not the sixth that answers the main question. Read it that way.

## The headline: the time question is probably not answerable from a desk

Be precise about how strong this is, because the approach rests on it.

**Two independent attempts have now failed to find a traceable number for how long any stage of US frontline
retail hiring takes.** The 9 Aug search found plenty of figures and not one with a source, all listed in section
D of [research-inputs.md](research-inputs.md). This run, under strict rules, found one candidate: a vendor's,
with no named company, no control group and a volume result rather than a tenure result. The audit killed it.

The honest caveat is that the angle built specifically to hunt this number died before reporting anything. So
this is two failed attempts rather than proof of absence.

Two failed attempts is enough to stop searching and change approach. It is not enough to say the number does not
exist anywhere.

So the funnel map in [funnel-map.md](funnel-map.md) is going to keep an empty time column, and that is a fact
about the world rather than a gap in our effort. Two consequences.

The slice for v1 gets chosen on structure and reasoning, with the assumption written down where it can be
attacked. That is what D-012 already says.

And the practitioner questions in [03-discovery/practitioner-questions.md](../03-discovery/practitioner-questions.md)
become more valuable, because they are the only route to this number.

## Three findings that change how we work

### 1. Improving retention shrinks hiring volume, and that is a pricing problem

Derenoncourt and Weil, NBER working paper 32546. Five large US retailers, each over 150,000 employees, 20
voluntary wage-floor events between 2014 and 2023, credit bureau payroll data covering about 18 million hourly
workers.

After those wage events, total headcount rose, by 1.25% across all events and 4.62% at the $15 events. But
year-on-year growth in new hires **fell significantly**, with coefficients of -0.860, -0.798 and -1.123, all
significant at the 1% level.

Headcount went up and gross hiring went down, because the gain came from fewer people leaving rather than more
people joining.

**Why this matters to us and not to the premise debate.** If our product is priced per hire, per requisition or
per applicant, then success reduces our own revenue. Both incumbents are enterprise-quote-only with no
published pricing, so we cannot see how they handle it. This is a Phase 5 business model question and it is now
on the list as Q-030.

*Confirmed by the audit against the paper's own Table 2, with all three percentages re-derived.*

### 2. The buyer may have no baseline to measure us against

Walmart employs roughly 1.6 million US associates, about 92% of them hourly, and its FY2026 10-K discloses no
turnover, retention or attrition rate anywhere. The auditor searched the full filing. The word turnover appears
only as inventory turnover.

Costco does publish a number, about 94% retention in the US and Canada for fiscal 2025. But it is defined as
retention "for employees who have been with us for at least one year," which excludes the first year, which is
where the churn is.

**Why this matters.** We already knew neither Fountain nor Paradox claims tenure. This adds a harder version:
even a buyer who wanted to hold us to a retention number may not have one. If v1 promises anything about
retention, we may have to establish the baseline ourselves as part of the deployment. That is a design
requirement, and it is the same requirement D-012 already produced for different reasons.

*Verified by direct search of the filings.*

### 3. Stable scheduling did not improve retention in the one US retail experiment that tested it

This one corrects me rather than adding something.

The Gap Inc. randomised trial is the flagship US retail scheduling experiment. 28 stores, 19 treatment and 9
control, San Francisco and Chicago, November 2015 to August 2016. Kesavan, Lambert, Williams and Pendem,
Management Science 2022.

**Overall turnover and overall retention were unchanged.** Turnover fell among more experienced associates, and
average tenure at quitting in treatment stores fell 10.8 months from a base of 25.5 months. That is a
composition shift, meaning newer people became the ones leaving. The productivity gain the study did find, 5.1%
from sales up 3.3% and labour down 1.8%, is attributed to keeping seasoned staff.

Separately, Seattle's Secure Scheduling Ordinance evaluation measured real improvements in predictability and
wellbeing and estimated **no effect on turnover, quits or tenure.** The auditor tested that negative
specifically and it holds.

What is left linking scheduling to turnover is observational panel data, 1,725 US hourly retail and food service
workers from The Shift Project, whose own abstract hedges to "these associations."

**Why this matters.** TODO item 4 and Q-024 carry my hypothesis that fair workweek enforcement makes hiring
speed newly expensive, described there as possibly "the what changed answer this project has been missing." The
enforcement part still stands. Chipotle settled with New York City for $20 million and Starbucks for about $39
million. But the link from scheduling stability to retention is a null in the one US retail trial that tested
it, so the hypothesis is weaker than I wrote it, and it should not be a load-bearing part of any pitch.

*Refuted and corrected by the audit. The original research had claimed the Gap trial never measured turnover at
all, which is false.*

## One finding worth knowing but not acting on

A CEO letter asking store managers to do what they could about quitting cut quits by a fifth to a quarter. The
effect lasted nine months, faded, and came back after a reminder. No effect on sales. Friebel, Heinz and
Zubanov, Management Science 2022, 68(1):211-229.

The chain is in an Eastern European country, not the US, so it does not transfer directly. It is here because
it says something about attention rather than capability: managers could move the number without new tools or
new budget, and did not keep doing it.

## The scale caution, which the synthesiser flagged as the thing to carry forward

Every good number in this set is a percentage cut off a very high base. A 15% reduction against roughly 60%
annual store attrition still leaves roughly 51%.

Nothing found here changes the order of magnitude of frontline retail churn. Worth holding on to before writing
any claim about what a product will do to it.

## What this does not say

It does not say the problem is not worth solving. That is closed by D-012 and this file does not reopen it.

It does not say what to build. Every finding above is either a constraint on how we price, a constraint on what
we can promise, or a correction to something I wrote earlier.

## Sources and their tiers

**Strongest, and the best number in the whole set.** Derenoncourt and Weil, NBER w32546. US, retail, frontline
hourly, about 18 million workers, credible design, audited and re-derived. Monthly separation rates for hourly
workers under $30 an hour fell 6.57%, 8.73% and 15.33% relative to the pre-period mean across the three event
types.

**Solid but not US.** The referral programme trial across all 238 stores of an Eastern European grocery chain,
pre-registered as AEARCTR-0000964. Attrition down 15% to 20%, durable across 13 months, labour costs down up to
2.8%. The decomposition is the paper's own and it is the interesting part: about 5% of the saving came from
referred hires staying longer, and about 95% of the profit gain came from the programme improving retention of
existing staff who were not referred. The authors' best-supported explanation is that workers value being
involved in hiring. No US replication was found.

**Company self-report, kept in its own tier.** Home Depot's roughly $1 billion annualised compensation
investment from Q1 fiscal 2023, filed with the SEC, covering wage, benefits, training and career development.
Sam's Club's claim of turnover down 1,700 basis points year on year, which has no published base rate,
population or method, and where the pay plan had been live only about 13 weeks by the end of the quarter being
compared.

**Killed by the audit.** About twenty claims. They are recorded in section D of
[research-inputs.md](research-inputs.md) so nobody rediscovers them and treats them as new.
