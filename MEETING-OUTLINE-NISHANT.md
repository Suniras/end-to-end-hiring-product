## Section 2. The four scope decisions (6 min)

Purpose: show that scope was narrowed deliberately, and that the reasoning survives inspection.

Present them in this order, because it goes from most to least likely to be questioned.

**Attract is out.** Lead with the reason, not the conclusion. The external data slot that would make it
valuable is already owned by Lightcast, Indeed, LinkedIn Talent Insights and ADP. The output is advice with
no forcing function, so it can be ignored without anything breaking. And a customer spreadsheet is
retrospective and non-exclusive, because the applicant tracking vendor holds the same records.

Then say what data would have been needed, because he may ask: per-channel outcome data joined from source
through to 90-day retention, across locations, over a full seasonal cycle. That does not exist in one place
at most retailers unless you were in the flow while it happened.

Then volunteer the counterargument yourself: land with light analytics, expand into the flow. Say why you
did not take it. Volunteering the strongest objection to your own decision is the single most useful thing
you can do in this meeting.

**Management roles are out of v1.** Hourly and management hiring are different jobs, different people,
different timescales, and Korn Ferry's numbers show they behave differently after hire too, at 75.8% against
17.7% turnover. Then say the cost out loud: management is where the money usually is, and we are excluding
the higher-value half.

**Nothing from NuAnchor transfers to the product.** Worth saying plainly since he owns it. Domain logic is
zero overlap, patterns are copyable by anyone, craft affects how we work rather than what we build. Platform
reuse is a Phase S1 engineering question, not a product one.

**Phase 1 runs on second-hand evidence.** State the cost rather than letting him find it: the Phase 1 gate
cannot be passed without practitioner conversations, so everything in the diagnosis carries an assumption
label until then. This sets up your main ask in section 5.

## Section 3. The finding that challenges our own premise (10 min)

Purpose: this is the meat. Handle it as a finding, not a confession.

**Frame it before you deliver it.** Something like: we ran a research sweep on why nobody has fixed this,
had every claim audited by a separate pass that fetched the sources, and it came back with something that
puts pressure on our own starting assumption. Finding this in week one rather than month six is the point of
doing it this way.

Then the substance:

- Central talent acquisition and store managers describe different problems.
- TA describes latency. A named 7-Eleven talent leader says they could not reach people fast enough and
  candidates were hired down the street first. That is exactly what Fountain, Paradox and Cue are built for,
  which is why speed tooling keeps selling.
- Store managers, in their own words, say they could not hire because they were not given enough payroll
  hours, and that pay levels made roles unfillable. None complain that applications were too slow.
- Across every vertical where churn genuinely fell, the mechanism was wages or schedule stability, never
  hiring software. A Gap randomised trial found schedule stability raised sales about 7%. California's
  fast-food wage floor cut separations. Amazon's answer to warehouse turnover was raising pay.
- And the line to land: every vendor claim in this category is about time-to-hire. Not one claims tenure.

**Then immediately give the caveats, before he does.** The store-manager evidence is public employee
reviews, which lean toward complaint. One audit found part of that angle had read warehouse hiring as store
hiring. So this is a strong signal and not a proof, and five practitioner conversations would settle it.

**Then the consequence, which is the part that matters.** If hiring speed is not what binds at store level,
the risk is not losing a deal to Fountain. It is winning a deal and having nobody use the product. That
looks like success for a year and then the renewal dies quietly. His own brief already warns about this.

## Section 5. The three asks (5 min)

Purpose: this is what the meeting is actually for. Everything above earns the right to ask.

**Ask one: five people who hire retail staff in the US.** This is the biggest risk to the project and it has
no date against it. Say the specific reason it matters now: the research found that the independent evidence
base for this question is close to empty, because nearly every source with real retail frontline specificity
turned out to be vendor marketing or single reviews. Desk research has a ceiling and we are near it. Ask for
names, a route to names, or a clear no so you can plan around it.

**Ask two: does NuAisle sell to big-box or not, and if not, where does this product sit.** He confirmed
NuAisle skips big-box and mass retail. High-volume frontline hiring concentrates there. So either this sits
outside NuAisle or the target is mid-market with a few hundred stores. Those are different products and Phase
4 and 5 both inherit the answer.

**Ask three: is there an organisational level between store manager and central TA that holds both volume
and authority.** Explain why it matters in one line: aviation concentrates half its recruiting in twenty of
two hundred stations so a central team can own it, retail spreads volume evenly so nobody holds both volume
and authority. If a district or regional level holds both, that is the buyer and the pilot site.

## Section 6. Questions, and one thing to tell him (rest of the time)

QUESTIONS-FOR-NISHANT.md has twelve, ordered by how much the answer changes the plan. The first four are
the ones above plus the payroll-hours question.

The thing to tell him rather than ask: the seasonal surge his brief names as a core driver has shrunk.

whether it is a trend.

Say it because it is better heard from you now than discovered later.

## Pushback you should expect, and where the answer lives

**"So is this project dead?"** No, and do not get defensive. The answer is that the value would have to come
from somewhere other than speed, and we do not yet know where. That is what Phase 1 is for. What would be
dead is a plan to ship a faster screening agent and expect adoption.

**"We already built this for Unifi, just do it for retail."** This is the likeliest push and you need your
own answer ready before the room. The material to draw on: Fountain already ships an agentic screening
layer, so it is a table stake rather than a differentiator. And the research says the store manager, who
would have to use it, has a different binding constraint. Building it quickly gets us to a product nobody
adopts. Your concentration observation is the sharpest thing you have here: aviation could be sold to a
central team because volume concentrates, retail cannot because it does not.

**"Why is this taking so long?"** Four days, Phase 0 closed, Phase 1 open, 11 decisions and 28 questions
logged, and a premise problem found before anything was built. That is the answer.

**"Just pick something and start."** Ask what he wants to have learned in 90 days. If the answer is whether
retailers will pay, that needs the diagnosis. If the answer is whether we can build it, Unifi already
answered that.

**Anything about compliance or security.** You deprioritised it deliberately and the reason is sound. If
pushed, the research supports you: enforcement is weaker than the statutes suggest, few audits have been
published under New York's law and Colorado repealed its duty before it took effect. But note that at Unifi
compliance shaped the product rather than following it, so it returns at Phase 7 as a requirement.

## What not to do

- Do not present a problem statement you have not written. An honest gap beats a confident guess.
- Do not agree a timeline in the room. You do not have a scope yet, so any date is invented.
- Do not accept a scope verbally. If something gets decided, write it down and read it back before you
  leave.
- Do not oversell Unifi. It went live, it was three screening questions producing pass or in-review, and it
  was contracted through TalentOS rather than Unifi directly. All of that is in the repo and he may know it
  better than you.

---

## What a good outcome looks like

You leave with a route to practitioners, an answer on big-box, and either agreement or correction on the
premise challenge.

You do not leave with "go build something." If that is where it lands, the meeting did not work regardless
of how it felt in the room, because you would be building against a problem statement that does not exist
yet.

One more thing worth noticing. The strongest thing you have going into this room is not the research. It is
that you found the problem with your own premise and are saying it out loud unprompted. Most people bury
that until someone else finds it.
